# Writing Human Prose

## Overview

Two jobs at once: write nonfiction that is engaging and well-sourced, and strip the
tells that make prose read as machine-generated. The second is mostly subtraction. AI
prose is not usually wrong, it is over-balanced, over-tidy, and over-polished in
recognizable ways. Cut those patterns and ground every claim and the writing reads human.

Core test: read each sentence aloud. If you would not say it to a knowledgeable friend,
cut or rewrite it.

## When to use

- Any longform nonfiction: essays, history, explainers, articles, posts.
- Documentation, READMEs, reports, marketing or web copy meant for people.
- Editing or "de-slopping" a draft (yours or a model's) so it does not read as AI.
- Not for: code, data, terse status notes, or structured reference tables.

## Nonfiction craft

- **Open on something concrete**: an image, an episode, a sharp fact. Never a
  definition, a mission statement, or "In this piece".
- **One thread per section.** Cut anything that does not serve it.
- **Lead with the idea, bring history in as evidence.** Go chronological only when the
  argument needs the sequence.
- **Show with specifics.** Concrete nouns and real detail beat abstraction.
- **Vary sentence length on purpose.** Let a short sentence land after a long one.
- **Quote sparingly.** Each quotation (a line, a slogan, a historian) must earn its place.
- **Close on a concrete point or a forward turn**, never a restatement of what you said.

## Sourcing discipline (for anything factual)

- Nothing from memory. Every date, name, and causal claim traces to a real source.
- Prefer primary and authoritative secondary sources; cite them.
- Where the record is contested or thin, say so and attribute. Flag genuine uncertainty
  rather than smoothing it over. Honesty beats tidiness.
- Separate fact from interpretation. Interpretation is allowed if labelled as such.

## The AI-tells blacklist (cut on sight)

- **Em dashes.** Never. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses.
- **Balance templates**: "not just X, but Y", "it isn't X, it's Y", "not only ... but
  also", and bare antithesis ("what had been a sneer became a banner").
- **Summary / lesson kickers**: ending by converting the point into a tidy takeaway
  ("a neat lesson in how language works", "a reminder that...", "In the end...").
- **Rule-of-three padding**: "a bandit, a rustler, a cutthroat" when one noun is right.
- **Trailing -ing significance clauses**: "...smoothed almost entirely out of memory",
  "...marking a turning point", tacked on to imply weight.
- **Banned words**: delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, robust,
  cutting-edge, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, paramount,
  transformative, elevate, embark, harness, ever-evolving, testament, pivotal,
  resonate (filler), landscape/navigate (figurative).
- **Throat-clearing**: "here's the thing", "it's worth noting", "let me be clear",
  "in today's world", "at the end of the day".
- **Faux-insight setups**: "what nobody tells you", "here's what most people get wrong".
- **Importance puffery**: "marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament", "plays a
  vital role".
- **Weasel attribution**: "experts agree", "studies show" with no named source.
- **Fake-strong copulas**: "serves as / acts as / functions as" where "is" works.
- **Synonym cycling** to dodge repetition. Repeat the plain word if it is right.
- **Formatting slop**: emoji in headings, random bold, needless headers.

## One worked example

A real baseline passage on the word "Tory", with the tells marked, then fixed.

Before (AI tells):
> What had been a sneer became a banner, worn first with defiance and then with pride.
> The journey is a neat lesson in how language works: a word for a hunted Irishman
> became the proud name of a governing party.

Problems: antithesis template ("sneer became a banner"), rule-of-three rhythm, and a
summary-lesson kicker.

After:
> The targets kept the insult. By the next century "Tory" was the settled name of a
> British governing party, and almost no one who used it remembered the hunted Irishman
> behind it.

## Red flags, stop and rewrite

- The sentence pivots on "not X but Y" or an antithesis.
- The paragraph ends by telling the reader what to take away.
- You reached for a word on the banned list.
- There is an em dash.
- A factual claim has no source behind it.

## Final checks

1. Read it aloud. Cut anything you would not say.
2. `grep` for em dashes. Expect zero.
3. Scan for the banned words and the templates above.
4. Every fact is sourced or explicitly flagged as interpretation or uncertain.
