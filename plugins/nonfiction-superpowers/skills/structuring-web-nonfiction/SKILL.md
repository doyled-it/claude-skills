# Structuring Web Nonfiction

## Overview

Rules for the shape of a web piece, not the sentences. The web rewards front-loading,
scannability, and honest data, and each form (science, data, argument, archive, guide) has
its own moves. Every rule here is checkable and carries a named source. For sentence craft
and cutting AI tells, use `writing-human-prose`. For the end-to-end process, use
`writing-a-researched-piece`.

## When to use

- Building any web article, explainer, data story, science or politics piece, topic page,
  or archive.
- Deciding structure, section order, headings, or how to present data and charts.
- Choosing the moves for a specific form.
- Not for: sentence-level clean-up (writing-human-prose), or fiction.

## Structure: every piece

- **Lede first.** Open with the single most important point, or a sharp illustrative
  anecdote. Direct lede for "tell me the news," anecdotal lede for "tell me a story"
  (Poynter).
- **Nut graf by paragraph 2 or 3.** One paragraph saying what the piece is about and why it
  matters now: it justifies the piece, bridges from the lede, and shows why it is timely
  (Scanlan, Poynter). Do not make the reader wait for the point.
- **Inverted pyramid.** Key fact first, then everything in descending importance so a reader
  can stop anywhere (NN/g). Test: if they read only paragraph 1, do they get the point?
- **Front-load every level** (NN/g F-pattern): descriptive headline, main point in the
  opening sentence, descriptive subheads, most-important sentence first in each paragraph,
  information-carrying first words in each sentence and link.

## Scannability: the web medium (NN/g)

- **Cut to about half the length you would write in print.** Screen reading is slower, and
  roughly 79% of users scan rather than read (Nielsen, "Be Succinct"; "How Users Read on the
  Web").
- **One idea per paragraph, the idea in the first sentence.** Scanners read only the opening
  sentence of a paragraph.
- **Descriptive subheads, not clever ones.** Reading only the headings should convey the gist
  (NN/g layer-cake). A heading is a label, not a slogan: name the section for what it contains.
  Kill the clever-title patterns, a balanced clause ("Naming the library is easy; everything
  after it is hard"), a colon slogan ("Location: necessary, and still not sufficient"), a
  trailing qualifier ("What the tools buy, once you know what to look up"), or a numbered motif
  ("A second wall: ..."). The fixes are plain: "Cold run: by rung", "Finding the function",
  "What tools add". The same ban applies to chart and callout titles that editorialize instead
  of stating the takeaway.
- **Front-load lines and subheads with the key word.** The first two words carry the scan
  (NN/g F-pattern).
- **Use bullets, short paragraphs, white space; line length about 50 to 75 characters**
  (NN/g chunking). When a paragraph is really a list (conditions, causes, steps, results),
  make it a list. Prefer three tight bullets to one dense sentence that stacks them with
  semicolons.
- **Do not narrate an interactive element.** If a control, chart, or widget already shows
  what it does, cut the paragraph that explains it. Give one line of orientation, then let the
  reader use it. A two-knob explorer does not need a paragraph describing its two knobs; show,
  do not tell.
- **Set recurring proper nouns in a distinct inline style** (a monospace pill, small caps, a
  subtle tint). Entities a reader scans for, model and product names, file paths, identifiers,
  should stand out from surrounding prose so the eye can find and compare them.
- **Cut marketese.** If deleting an adjective loses no fact, it was promotion (NN/g).
- **Link text is specific and names its destination, keyword first.** Never "click here,"
  "read more," or "learn more." Make each link label unique (NN/g, the "4 Ss").

## Data and charts (Knaflic, Cairo, Tufte, Data Journalism Handbook)

- **Name the relationship before you pick the chart** (comparison, change over time,
  distribution, composition). Default to bar and line; reach further only when they cannot
  carry the point (Knaflic; DJH).
- **Length and height encodings start the axis at zero** (every bar chart). Line charts may
  use a non-zero baseline if it is reasonable and clearly labeled (Cairo).
- **The title states the takeaway**, not a label like "Revenue by quarter" (Knaflic).
- **One accent color, everything else muted.** Color is a signal, not decoration (Knaflic).
- **Declutter:** kill gridlines, borders, 3D, and chartjunk; maximize the share of ink that
  encodes data (Tufte); label key points directly instead of forcing a legend (Knaflic).
- **Introduce every chart in the text beside it:** say what to look for, then show it (DJH).
- **Cite the data source on or under every chart** (who collected it, and when), and keep a
  method note so the chart is reproducible (DJH).
- **Show uncertainty** for anything estimated or projected (ranges, bands); do not draw a
  guess as a confident line (Cairo).
- **Give honest context:** per-capita where raw counts mislead, a fair time window, real
  baselines; do not cherry-pick the window (Cairo).
- **Alt text per chart:** "[chart type] of [data] where [the takeaway]" (Cesal, via
  Datawrapper and DJH).

## Explanation moves (Heath, Zimmer, The Open Notebook)

- **One core message, stated up front.** Three priorities means none (Heath, "Simple").
- **Open on a surprise or a knowledge gap**, not a summary (Heath, "Unexpected").
- **Concrete over abstract.** Swap category nouns for things the reader can picture; a named
  person beats an aggregate number (Heath, "Concrete").
- **Beat the curse of knowledge.** Define or cut every term an outsider would not share; have
  a non-expert mark what is unclear (Heath).
- **Explain the concept before the term,** then attach the label. Cut acronyms (Zimmer).
- **Anchor each hard idea to an accurate analogy on first mention,** and note where the
  analogy breaks down (The Open Notebook).
- **Match claims to evidence.** One study is not proof, correlation is not cause; say what is
  uncertain and what is nonetheless well established (Zimmer; The Open Notebook).

## Per-form rule-sets

Pick the form first. It sets the defaults.

**Essay or narrative explainer** (for example, a context piece, or a history that makes an
argument; the Cúlra page is this, not the archive form below)
- Let the structure rise from the material; do not stamp a template on it (McPhee).
- Write the lead first and treat it as a flashlight into the whole piece, a promise of its
  substance and style. The lead and the ending talk to each other (McPhee).
- Decide the ending before you write the middle. It answers the question the lead raised, and
  leaves the reader one thought they did not have before (McPhee; Zinsser).
- Default to chronology; switch to a thematic order only when it serves the argument, and
  expect tension between the two (McPhee).
- Hold unity of pronoun, tense, and mood; signal any shift with "but," "yet," or "however"
  (Zinsser).
- The ending should take the reader slightly by surprise yet seem exactly right. Give the
  last sentence as much thought as the first, and know when to stop (Zinsser).

**Science or research explainer** (for example, "what the research actually says")
- Lead with the finding's meaning and its level of certainty, not the methodology.
- Concept before term, no acronyms, accurate analogies.
- Verbs and hedges match the study's real strength. Do not overstate; one study is not proof.
- Verify facts against primary literature; do not outsource fact-checking to a single source
  (Zimmer).

**Data explainer** (for example, itemized, sourced numbers)
- Every number is itemized and sourced, with a visible method note.
- Follow the full data-and-charts block above.
- Give baselines and per-capita or real-terms context so a number means something.
- Lead with the headline number and what it means.

**Argument, analysis, or political**
- Label analysis and opinion; distinguish fact, unverified claim, and direct quote (SPJ).
- Steelman the other side first: restate it so they would endorse your summary, then rebut
  (Rapoport's Rules, via Dennett).
- Name things plainly; refuse euphemism that hides what happened (Orwell).
- Give anyone you criticize a chance to respond, and attribute precisely (SPJ).

**Cultural or exploratory archive** (the annotation or reference form; the lyric pages are
this, not the essay form above)
- Research before writing; cite every claim; flag genuine uncertainty instead of smoothing it.
- Keep fact, the subject's own framing, and your interpretation separate.
- Present difficult history as cited history; do not glorify.

**Practical or reference guide** (for example, an itinerary or a how-to)
- Front-load the actionable answer; order by the reader's task, not your narrative.
- Maximum scannability: tables, lists, clear headings. Cut everything that is not actionable.

Honesty flags from the research: the one-sentence lead word-count is a teaching heuristic,
not a verified AP rule; Vox "card stacks" are observed practice, not a published standard;
Rapoport's Rules should be checked against Dennett before quoting verbatim.
